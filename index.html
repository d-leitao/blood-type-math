How the score works

Pressure on a patient group is P = patients ÷ people who can donate to them. At 1.0 every compatible donor is needed for that group alone.

A donor’s score is θ + (1 − θ) × ΣP over the groups it can give to, where θ is the share of patients given their own type. Universal donors (O− red cells, AB plasma) also carry emergency use, before the patient’s type is known, and that slice comes out of everyone else’s share. The score is how often a type’s donors would need to give to meet demand, against the average donor. Total demand equals total donations, so scores average exactly 1.0. Above 1, the type runs short unless its donors give more often. Below 1, its donors could give less often, or give another component. Counting reach alone would overrate common pairings: A+ patients can already take blood from most people.

The two assumptions

Own type: the share of typed patients given their own type; the rest is shared across compatible donors. 0% is fully flexible. At 100% only emergency use separates the types.

Emergencies: the share of universal donors’ blood used before the patient’s type is known. It’s a share of their blood, not of all blood, so it stays realistic where they’re rare. A fixed share of all blood would put about 15 times the average load on O− donors in Japan.

Red cells: in England 16% of O− units went to emergencies, and O− is 7.8% of patients but 13% of red-cell stock (NHSBT 2018 audit). That implies about 80% own type. With the same emergency share, Australia (9% of people, over 15% of demand; Lifeblood) and the US (6.8% of donors, 12.1% of transfusions in 2023; AABB) imply 76–81%, and a 2024 audit in Victoria, Australia about 50% (Blood Matters).

Plasma: no audit found. About 73% matches Canada, where AB is 14% of plasma issued but about 3% of people (Canadian Blood Services). No published figures were found for Portugal.

The estimates were fitted together: 80% own type assumes 16% emergencies. With no emergencies, England’s figures fit about 69%.

Assumptions

  • Patients have the same type mix as the figures above, many of which come from donors. Real patients skew older, and donor pools differ by age and ethnicity.
  • Apart from emergencies, the shared part is spread evenly. Real substitution is lopsided (expiring units, special requirements), so the universal donors’ scores are the best anchored.
  • Much plasma becomes medicines, where type doesn’t matter, so the plasma view covers transfusion plasma only.
  • ABO and RhD only. Hospitals still crossmatch every unit, and other antigen systems matter for some patients.
