Blood type math

Who can give blood to whom, how common each type is, and which donations go furthest. In the compatibility matrix, rows are donors and columns are recipients. Every type label carries its share of the population, and the outer edges add up how many people each donor can reach and how large each recipient’s donor pool is. Which donations go furthest?

A donation goes further when it can reach patients who have few other donors to rely on. Counting how many people a type can give to overrates common pairings, because a group like A+ can already take blood from most of the population, so the model weighs each patient group by how hard it is to supply. Hospitals give most patients their own type, and the slider sets how much.

Step 1 · Pressure on each patient group
Pj = pj ÷ Poolj

The share of people with type j, divided by the share who can donate to them. At 1.0, every compatible donor is needed just to cover that group. Small values mean donors to spare.

Step 2 · Load on one donor
Vi = θ + (1 − θ) · Σj ∈ reach(i) Pj

θ is the share of each group’s need met with same-type blood, set below. The rest is split evenly across everyone who can donate to that group, so the sum adds up the pressure on every group type i can give to. V averages exactly 1.0 across the population, so 2.0 means twice the load of the average donor.

What the model assumes

  • Patients and donors are spread like the general population, and everyone donates at the same rate. In reality patients skew older, and donor pools differ by age and ethnicity.
  • One same-type share applies to every group, and the rest is spread evenly across compatible donors. Real substitution is lopsided: mostly O− in emergencies before a patient is typed, units about to expire, and patients with special requirements. O−’s score at about 70% is anchored to data; the other types’ scores are rougher.
  • Only ABO and RhD count, and every unit is worth the same. Plasma ignores RhD, so negative and positive types score the same for plasma. Much donated plasma is made into medicines, where blood type doesn’t matter, so the plasma view only applies to plasma used directly in transfusions.

About the numbers

Compatibility here covers ABO and RhD only. Hospitals still type and crossmatch every unit, and other antigen systems (Kell, Duffy, Kidd and more) matter for some patients. Plasma is matched on ABO alone.

Country figures come from national blood services as compiled in Blood type distribution by country on Wikipedia. Many are measured on donor populations rather than whole countries. Rows the source doesn’t total to 100% (the UK’s sums to 96%) are rescaled. Portugal’s figures trace to the Portuguese Blood and Transplantation Institute (IPST) and assume RhD is independent of ABO.
